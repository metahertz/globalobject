# Porting Ceph or OpenZFS fixes nothing

**None of GlobalObject's eleven maturity issues would be resolved by porting Ceph, OpenZFS, or both into Rust: the score is 0 of 11.** Three issues have a real fix that is a design idea you could borrow without copying any code: partial reads (7), forget/prune (8), and a little of the receipt problem (6). Two issues would get *worse* if ZFS code were adopted: crypto assurance (3) and metadata leakage (4). Licensing alone settles the question. Ceph's core is LGPL-2.1/3 and OpenZFS is CDDL-1.0, and a translated port stays under the original licence in the US, UK and EU, so neither fits a permissive-only policy. The cost settles it a second time. By Open Hub's COCOMO formula, Ceph (about 2.3M lines) and OpenZFS (about 0.5M lines) come to roughly 690 and 135 person-years, and the result would be a storage system with no production history, which is the opposite of mature. The two crates that really are pre-1.0, `rustic_core` and `iroh-blobs`, have cheap mitigations: pinning, vendoring, keeping them behind internal traits, the restic Go binary as a recovery fallback, and pinning or funding iroh-blobs' stable line. Each costs days, not decades.

How to read this report: claims with an inline link come from the research notes, which were collected on 2026-11-07. Statements marked **(inference)** are analysis by the researchers or by this report and have no direct source. docs.ceph.com, ceph.io, openzfs.github.io, openhub.net, opensource.org, spdx.org and despairlabs.com all refused connections from the research environment. Ceph and OpenZFS documentation was therefore read from the `.rst` and man-page sources in their GitHub repos. Several figures, marked "(search excerpt)", come from search-engine summaries of pages that could not be opened.

## Both systems assume trusted nodes, and that is the problem

Every one of the eleven issues comes from GlobalObject's starting assumption that "every peer is untrusted" (`docs/threat-model.md`). Ceph and ZFS were both built on the opposite assumption. **This is why porting fails (inference): rewriting in Rust changes the implementation language, not the threat model.**

Ceph is designed around one administrative domain whose daemons fail by crashing, not by lying. cephx "uses shared secret keys for authentication", so "both the client and the monitor cluster keep a copy of the client's secret key", and "Ceph Monitors and OSDs share a secret" ([Ceph architecture.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/reef/doc/architecture.rst)). Scrub is run by the OSDs themselves. They "compare the metadata of their own local objects against the metadata of the replicas" and check data "bit-for-bit, against their checksums" (same source). That catches bit rot, not an operator who lies. A search for Byzantine or malicious-OSD handling found no such mechanism. No source says outright that Ceph has no Byzantine fault tolerance, so that conclusion is a strong inference rather than a quotation. Red Hat's stretch-cluster guidance asks for at most **10 ms RTT between data sites** ([RHCS 9 stretch clusters](https://docs.redhat.com/en/documentation/red_hat_ceph_storage/9/html/administration_guide/stretch-clusters-for-ceph-storage), search excerpt). The project had already rejected Ceph on this basis in `docs/dependencies.md`. A "tiebreaker up to 100 ms" figure that circulates could not be confirmed.

OpenZFS assumes one trusted kernel that owns every disk, the allocator and the transaction groups. Its encryption protects data, not the structure of the pool. It "will not encrypt metadata related to the pool structure", including dataset and snapshot names, properties, **file size and holes**, and dedup tables ([zfs-load-key.8](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openzfs/zfs/master/man/man8/zfs-load-key.8)). Its encrypted send/recv path had corruption bugs reported from 2021 that were not fixed until 2025. Maintainer Alexander Motin said of the fix, "I don't think there is still a proper understanding of what's going on", and another contributor said he had "given up trying to fix native encryption issues" ([openzfs-docs#494](https://github.com/openzfs/openzfs-docs/issues/494)). No formal audit of ZFS encryption was found.

## Issue-by-issue: zero fixes, two regressions, three borrowable ideas

The verdicts below combine the separate Ceph and ZFS analyses in the notes. "Neither" means no code from either project, ported or used as-is, would resolve the issue. The verdicts are **inference** drawn from the cited findings.

| # | Issue | Ceph port | ZFS port | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `rustic_core` pre-1.0 | No | No | **Neither** | Ceph's RADOS/RGW/RBD have no client-side AEAD backup format. A Rust port of ZFS send/recv would be new 0.x code standing in for a large C subsystem. Both swap one immature dependency for a far larger one |
| 2 | `iroh-blobs`/`bao-tree` pre-1.0 | No | No | **Neither** | Neither system does verified streaming or content-addressed transfer. Ceph's checksums stay inside the OSD, and ZFS replication runs over ssh |
| 3 | Restic crypto unaudited | No (weaker) | No (worse) | **Neither** | Ceph RBD encryption is LUKS AES-XTS, "currently the only supported encryption mode" ([rbd-encryption.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/doc/rbd/rbd-encryption.rst)): no authentication tag, and the wrong layer. msgr2 AES-GCM protects transport only ([msgr2.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/doc/rados/configuration/msgr2.rst)). ZFS crypto has no audit found and a 2021–2025 bug record. Rewriting it in Rust would be custom crypto, which the project's own rule forbids |
| 4 | Pack sizes visible to hosts | No | No (worse) | **Neither** | Ceph doesn't hide object sizes from OSDs. ZFS leaves per-file size, holes and names in plaintext by design ([zfs-load-key.8](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openzfs/zfs/master/man/man8/zfs-load-key.8)). Raw send streams would leak more than restic packs. The fix is padding or fixed-size segments |
| 5 | Sybil replica dedupe at k=1 | No (harder) | No | **Neither** | Ceph trusts OSDs and has no proof of replication. CRUSH's deterministic placement would let an attacker grind IDs to land on chosen placement groups (inference). ZFS dedup is local, works only within one encryption family, and is the opposite of what's needed |
| 6 | Coarse read receipts | Idea only | No | **Neither** | Receipts are accounting between two untrusted parties, and neither system has anything like them. FastEC's stripe-unit granularity suggests making grants by range |
| 7 | Whole-pack reads only | Idea only | No | **Neither** (idea from Ceph) | Tentacle FastEC adds partial reads ([erasure-code.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/doc/rados/operations/erasure-code.rst)), but the code is tied to RADOS. ZFS send streams are sequential incremental chains, which is coarser than packs. The fix is range grants on top of iroh-blobs ranges plus mapping into systematic RS shards |
| 8 | No forget/prune | No | Idea only | **Neither** (idea from ZFS) | `rustic_core` already implements prune. What's missing is control-plane wiring: deleting pieces on hosts, credit and grace. ZFS's birth-txg and deadlist accounting is a good pattern, but incremental chains stored remotely can't be pruned |
| 9 | Audit detection rate / proof of possession | No | No | **Neither** | Ceph scrub and ZFS scrub are both self-checks by a trusted node. GlobalObject's BLAKE3 range audits are already stronger against a lying host. Measuring detection rate is a simulator task |
| 10 | NAT traversal / relays untested | No | No | **Neither** | RADOS assumes routable, low-latency addresses (inference, unsourced). ZFS has no networking. What's missing is testing, not code |
| 11 | Control plane lacks TLS and HA | No | No | **Neither** | msgr2 is a bespoke protocol, not TLS. MON Paxos is the right pattern but its code is bound to Ceph's map types ([architecture.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/reef/doc/architecture.rst)). The fix is `rustls`, plus Postgres HA (Patroni/CloudNativePG) or `openraft` |

Doing both ports gains nothing extra, because no issue falls in the gap between the two systems. Each issue is either outside both systems' threat models (3, 4, 5, 6, 9) or outside their scope altogether (1, 2, 10, 11). One thing outside the list is worth noting. Repair bandwidth over home uplinks is a real cost, and Ceph's LRC and CLAY plugins address it. At k=8, m=4, d=11, CLAY repairs 1 GiB by downloading **2.75 GiB instead of 8 GiB**. But "support for this plugin will be removed in the Vampire release" ([erasure-code-clay.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/doc/rados/operations/erasure-code-clay.rst)). LRC halves the number of helpers needed for a single-loss repair at the price of extra storage overhead ([erasure-code-lrc.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/doc/rados/operations/erasure-code-lrc.rst)). That overhead would come straight out of the 267–400 GB-per-TB figure that already threatens demand.

## A translated port inherits LGPL or CDDL, so only clean-room work is open

**Licence verdict: a line-by-line Rust port of either codebase fails the policy. Clean-room reimplementation from papers and specifications is the only permitted route.** This is not legal advice.

Ceph's COPYING file gives the default for `Files: *` as "LGPL-2.1 or LGPL-3". CRUSH, msgr, monitors, BlueStore, crimson and the LRC, SHEC and CLAY plugins have no separate stanza, so the default applies to them ([ceph/ceph COPYING](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/COPYING)). `src/crush/mapper.c` carries an explicit LGPL-2.1 header ([mapper.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/src/crush/mapper.c)). The only Rust CRUSH crate, `libcrush`, says its sources "were extracted from Ceph on January 2017 and are licensed under LGPL-2.1" ([libcrush README](https://docs.rs/crate/libcrush/latest/source/README.md)), and crates.io lists it as GPL-3.0-or-later OR LGPL-2.1-or-later. A few vendored files are permissive, notably the BSD-style Jerasure plugin sources, Intel's BSD-3 CRC32C and the public-domain `ceph_hash.cc`, but none of them addresses an issue on the list.

OpenZFS is CDDL "unless otherwise noted" ([COPYRIGHT](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openzfs/zfs/master/COPYRIGHT)). CDDL §1.9 defines Modifications to include "any new file that contains any part of the Original Software", and §3.2 says those Modifications "are governed by the terms of this License" ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openzfs/zfs/master/LICENSE)). A translated ZFS file therefore fails the policy twice: CDDL is not MPL-2.0, and the file would be modified.

The law treats translation as a derivative work in all three jurisdictions that matter here. US 17 U.S.C. §101 lists "a translation" first among derivative works ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101)). UK CDPA s.21 expressly covers conversion "into a different computer language or code" ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/21)). EU Directive 2009/24 Art. 4(1)(b) reserves "translation, adaptation, arrangement" to the rightsholder ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/2009/24/article/4/data.html)). Clean-room work remains possible because, under *SAS v World Programming* (CJEU, 2012), functionality and data-file formats are not protected by copyright, and the defendant there won partly because it never had access to the source ([SCL report](https://www.scl.org/2451-sas-institute-inc-v-world-programming-ltd-report-and-analysis/)). uutils coreutils turns this into practice: contributors must not read GPL or LGPL source, and use the manuals as a behavioural reference instead ([uutils CONTRIBUTING mirror](https://git.joshthomas.dev/mirrors/coreutils/src/commit/3382623bbcead96feb9311fd22d3d61ba76a4d7e/CONTRIBUTING.md)).

Patents are a separate risk that permissive copyright does not remove. StreamScale asserted US 8,683,296 against GF-Complete and Jerasure 2.0, and Plank withdrew both libraries ([Plank, UT-EECS-14-721](https://library.eecs.utk.edu/files/ut-eecs-14-721.pdf)). In 2015 it also wrote to Ubuntu naming Ceph's jerasure plugin ([Ubuntu technical-board](https://lists.ubuntu.com/archives/technical-board/2015-April/002100.html)). **(Inference)** Any fast GF(2^8) Reed–Solomon carries some exposure, including the `reed-solomon-*` crates GlobalObject already uses. The patent's expiry status and claim scope were not checked, and neither was the patent status of CRUSH, LRC or CLAY.

## Porting costs hundreds of person-years and resets maturity to zero

**Effort estimate (inference from sourced line counts):** Open Hub lists Ceph at about 2.34M lines ([Open Hub, search excerpt](https://openhub.net/accounts/rossturk/projects)), and Coverity Scan analyses 493,098 lines of OpenZFS ([Coverity Scan](https://scan.coverity.com/projects/openzfs-zfs)). Open Hub's Basic COCOMO formula, 2.4 × KLOC^1.05 person-months ([Open Hub method](https://openhub.net/p/git/estimated_cost)), gives about **690 person-years for Ceph and 135 for OpenZFS**. These numbers estimate writing from scratch. A port reuses the design and so would cost less, but the order of magnitude holds. The best real-world comparison is fish: 57K lines of C++ became about 75K lines of Rust through 2,604 commits by more than 200 authors over almost two years ([fish rustport blog](https://fishshell.com/blog/rustport/)). Ceph is about 40 times that size, and distributed-storage code is harder than shell code. Ceph's own team is still shipping Crimson, its C++/Seastar rewrite of the OSD, as "mainly for early testing and experimentation" in Tentacle ([Tentacle release](https://ceph.io/en/news/blog/2025/v20-2-0-tentacle-released), search excerpt). Redox gave up on ZFS because its "monolithic design" made it "slow to implement" ([redox-os/tfs](https://github.com/redox-os/tfs)). Porting only the smaller pieces doesn't help either. A CRUSH-sized algorithm is cheap to port, but per the table it fixes none of the issues.

The deeper point is that **a port does not inherit the original's production history** (inference). The Rust ports that shipped widely still hit behaviour-mismatch bugs when first deployed at scale. On Ubuntu 25.10, md5sum differences in uutils broke Makeself archives ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Ubuntu-25.10-Coreutils-Makeself)), and a `date` flag that was never wired up broke unattended-upgrades ([It's FOSS](https://itsfoss.com/news/ubuntu-25-10-rust-automatic-upgrade-bug/)). sudo-rs shipped two moderate CVEs ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/sudo-rs-security-ubuntu-25.10)). A storage system fails silently by losing data, so a Rust Ceph or ZFS with zero field hours would be the least mature component in GlobalObject. Automated translation does not shortcut this. DARPA TRACTOR's programme manager says LLM output is "not error-free" ([Dark Reading](https://darkreading.com/application-security/darpa-aims-to-ditch-c-code-move-to-rust)), and no 2026 results were found.

## Six designs worth borrowing clean-room

None of these needs any source code, and each maps onto an existing GlobalObject gap (all inference, built on the cited mechanisms). **Ceph FastEC's stripe-unit layout** shows how to serve partial reads. Tentacle's FastEC reads "only the minimal data to honour the client request" ([erasure-code.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/ceph/main/doc/rados/operations/erasure-code.rst)). For GlobalObject that becomes range-scoped grants plus a pack-offset-to-shard mapping over the systematic RS code and iroh-blobs' existing verified range requests, which addresses issue 7 and narrows the receipt problem in issue 6. **ZFS's birth-txg and deadlist accounting** suggests recording, for each pack, the earliest and latest snapshot that references it, so that the control plane can tell which pieces a `forget` frees without a full scan. Issue 8 still comes down mainly to calling rustic's existing prune. **ZFS's keyless scrub**, which can run "without the encryption keys being loaded" ([zfs-load-key.8](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openzfs/zfs/master/man/man8/zfs-load-key.8)), confirms the existing choice to audit ciphertext without owner keys. **LRC-style local parity** is the cheapest way to cut repair traffic over home uplinks, but it costs storage overhead. CLAY should be avoided because it is deprecated and needs many non-contiguous sub-chunk reads. **The CRUSH failure-domain hierarchy and placement groups** are already credited in `docs/architecture.md`. CRUSH's remaining feature, that anyone holding the map can compute placement, is a liability against Sybils, so placement should stay server-side and random. Rendezvous (HRW) hashing with a server-held secret salt would be a simple alternative if deterministic recomputation is ever wanted. Finally, **the MON quorum pattern** argues for operator-run HA on the control plane, implemented with existing OSS such as Postgres with Patroni, or `openraft`, never as a Paxos port.

## Recommendation: mitigate the two pre-1.0 crates for days, not decades

Don't port Ceph or OpenZFS, whether in whole, in part, or together. Spend the effort on the specific fixes in the table: padding (4), EC or proof-of-replication research (5), range grants (6, 7), wiring up rustic prune (8), a detection-rate simulator (9), real-network NAT tests (10), and `rustls` with Postgres HA (11). For issue 3, commission a scoped review. The NCC Group review of RustCrypto AES-GCM and ChaCha20Poly1305 took **5 person-days** ([NCC Group](https://www.nccgroup.com/research/public-report-rustcrypto-aesgcm-and-chacha20pluspoly1305-implementation-review/)), which is a realistic model for funding a review of restic's crypto as rustic implements it.

For the two crates that really are pre-1.0, the evidence shows that "0.x" in Rust mostly signals API churn, not unreliability. Cargo treats 0.x minor bumps as breaking ([users.rust-lang.org](https://users.rust-lang.org/t/why-are-the-version-numbers-on-crates-io-so-low/8284)), and `rustls` 0.23 has 1.09B downloads. The real warning signs are explicit maintainer disclaimers and thin adoption. **`iroh-blobs`** is at 0.104.1, and its README says the current line "is not yet considered production quality. For now, if you need production quality, use iroh-blobs 0.35" ([iroh-blobs README](https://github.com/n0-computer/iroh-blobs)). iroh itself reached 1.4.1, but blobs has not followed despite the roadmap's H2 2025 target ([iroh roadmap](https://iroh.computer/roadmap)). The cheap mitigations, in rising order of cost, are: pin exact versions and commit `Cargo.lock`; `cargo vendor` with cargo-deny or cargo-vet; keep iroh-blobs types out of GlobalObject's public API behind an internal trait, as already done for the rustic backend ([api-guidelines#267](https://github.com/rust-lang/api-guidelines/issues/267)); decide whether pinning 0.35 is worth its older API; and offer n0 sponsorship or contributions towards blobs 1.0. **`rustic_core`** is at 0.13.0 with 114K downloads and no published 1.0 roadmap (crates.io API, 2026-11-07). **(Inference)** Its risk lies in the API, not in the data, because the on-disk format is restic's, so the Go `restic` binary can serve as an independent recovery path. A CI job that restores GlobalObject-written repositories with upstream restic would turn that inference into a tested guarantee, and is likely the cheapest maturity gain available. Forking stays a fallback if upstream stalls.

The notes have gaps that do not change the verdict. Licences of the `ceph`/`async-ceph` binding crates, ISA-L's licence and patent grant, the status of US 8,683,296, any patents on CRUSH, LRC or CLAY, final OpenZFS-on-Windows status, and bupstash were not verified. The Proxmox Backup Server licence (believed to be AGPL-3.0) was also left unverified.

## Conclusion

The question assumed that "pre-1.0" meant "needs a more mature engine underneath". The research shows that GlobalObject's issues are not about engine maturity at all. Most of them are adversarial-host problems, which Ceph and ZFS deliberately leave out of scope. The rest are integration and testing work in code the project already has. Porting mature C/C++ yields immature Rust, with an incompatible licence and threat model attached. Maturity comes from field hours and audits, not from where the code came from.

The practical upshot is that GlobalObject's prior-art debt to Ceph and ZFS is already paid in ideas: the failure-domain hierarchy, placement groups and keyless scrub. Three more ideas are worth taking, namely stripe-unit partial reads, deadlist-style reclaim accounting and local-parity repair. The highest-leverage maturity work is to test against the real format and the real network, not to rewrite anything: a cross-implementation restore test with restic, a NAT field trial, and a funded crypto review.
